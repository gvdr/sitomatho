What this page is

The importance scores in this journal come from two language models. To learn what their scores mean, we sent them {{ cal.mathlib_count }} theorems that people wrote: classical results, facts nobody would call a theorem, and theorems drawn at random from the Mathlib files our generator has explored. Each was presented exactly as a result of this journal is, with the same prompt, the same novelty certificate, its name removed, and the list of lemmas its real proof cites. The referees were not told. This page is not satire.

What Referee B makes of the classics

Referee B recognises every one of these theorems, usually by name, and still spreads them across most of the scale. Reading its reports, three habits explain the spread.

  1. It grades the proof it is shown. A classical statement that Mathlib derives in a step or two from a stronger lemma is treated as a corollary of that lemma, and scored like one.
  2. It dislikes the dress of formalisation. Typeclass hypotheses, coercions and extended metrics count against a statement, however standard they are in Lean.
  3. It never pays for novelty here. It knows these results are old and says so, even in its highest marks. A high score is for stature.

How to read this

  • Every theorem carried the certificate of a published result, including "exact? failed" and "not routine". For the trivial facts that certificate is false.
  • The lemma list and the proof-to-statement ratio come from the theorem's real Mathlib proof. The journal's own results carry an inflated ratio, by design.
  • The prompt tells the referee that the journal publishes unimportant theorems. It gave high marks regardless.
  • Referee B answers at a high temperature. On the classics its three answers can differ by several points; on our results they almost never differ.
  • This is one run. The theorems and the script are in the repository (scripts/calibration_set.json, scripts/calibrate_referees.py).

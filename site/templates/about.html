Kernel-Checked Babel: a generator of verified, unimportant theorems

Giulio Valentino Dalla Riva · Nov 23, 2026
Editorial Transparency Note

{{ journal.title }} ({{ journal.short_title }}) is a tongue-in-cheek web experiment and parody of automated discovery claims, not a real research journal. Every theorem published here is genuinely type-checked by the Lean 4 kernel against a pinned Mathlib release using only standard axioms (propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound), while every eponym, referee report, importance score, and press release is satire. Identifiers use the site's own scheme (AKCM-2026-NNNN), never DOIs or arXiv numbers.

The note

Generating mathematical results that are formally correct, almost certainly new, and barely intelligible to us is NOT HARD. In 1960 Hao Wang had an IBM 704 prove the roughly 220 propositional theorems of Principia Mathematica in about three minutes, and close to 400 of its first-order theorems overall [1]. Today, anyone can forward-chain from Lean's Mathlib and produce unlimited, kernel-checked novelty. It is Borges's Library of Babel [2] with a type checker.

What is hard is generating results that are "important". But "important" is not an abstract, formal metric. It is a value judgement: Poincaré described invention as discernment and choice [3], and Hardy ranked theorems by their "seriousness", their generality and depth [4], a criterion recently analysed explicitly as a value judgement [16]. For now it is a judgement socially constructed within the mathematical community. As Thurston put it, the question is not how mathematicians prove theorems but how they advance human understanding [5].

Every attempt to formalise "interestingness" encodes somebody's taste. That runs from Lenat's AM [6] and the survey by Colton, Bundy and Walsh [7] to Bengio and Malkin's compression view [8]. It includes proposals that score a theorem by its proof difficulty relative to its statement length [9, 10]. These are defensible proxies, and still choices.

Sure, we can have AIs that develop results that are important to them. Self-motivated conjecture-and-prove systems already do a version of this [11, 12, 13]. Do we care? Dunno. I suppose anything can be of interest.

Is anything actually new?

Yes. Random chains of valid steps reach new mathematics in every checkable sense of "new", as long as each result carries a certificate of which sense it meets. "New" is graded, and each grade has a test Lean can run:

A forward chain of k random steps gives Level 0 almost surely. Gating on Level 1 alone is cheap and guarantees nothing is a disguised single library lemma. Levels 2 and 3 are measured and printed on each theorem's page as badges, not used to discard. Levels 0 to 3 are formal, so the machine can climb them; Levels 4 and 5 are the note's point.

One honest caveat: every derivable statement is already implied by the axioms, so novelty is always relative to a library and a searcher. It is a cheaper judgement than importance, but it is a judgement too.

Method in plain words

  1. Random forward walk (Generator A): a Lean 4 metaprogram opens a random Mathlib theorem into a local context Γ, repeatedly applies unifying Mathlib lemmas (biased toward crossing distinct namespaces), shrinks away unused hypotheses, and reads off the resulting theorem and proof term.
  2. Novelty gate & badges: every candidate must survive exact? within a heartbeat budget (Level 1). We then measure whether simp or aesop can prove it (Level 2) and record its post-shrinking proof-to-statement AST ratio (Level 3).
  3. Baroque proof convolution: to optimise the Level 3 interestingness proxy directly, each verified proof term is passed through a semantics-preserving convoluter (Babel/Convolute.lean) that inserts vacuous order dichotomies, unreachable contradiction branches, symmetric conjunction–disjunction round-trips, and inductive case-analysis ceremonies—inflating proof length with zero extra axioms.
  4. Standalone verification: each result is emitted as an independent Result.lean file, compiled by lake env lean, and checked with #print axioms to allow only propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound.
  5. Editorial parody: two LLM referees independently classify the result, assign an importance score (0–10) and MSC 2020 code, and write two-sentence reports—disagreements published in full. A namer assigns an eponym from an invented AI model family (checked against a blocklist of real models and mathematicians, version-bumped each issue, and retired to "legacy" at 50 results) plus a mandatory buzzword press release that never explains the mathematics.
